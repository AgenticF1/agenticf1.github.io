[Recap] Norris’s pit stop at Spa was slow, and the window that would have fixed it went unused

Piastri pitted lap 20, under a VSC, for 2.8 seconds. Norris pitted lap 30, clean, for 7.8. Read the stationary time on its own and it sounds like a good stop. Set it against the other 27 real pit stops that happened at Spa that day and it is one of the slowest in the field. We ran the counterfactuals for both mistakes, the slow stop and the missed window, and found the second one cost far more than the first.

2026-10-02AgenticF1

Part one

What happened

Norris qualified third and started thirteenth, a ten-place drop from an engine-part change.

He moved up to sixth by lap 13, then passed his own teammate right before the virtual safety car, before being repassed. Call it yo-yoing.

Then the pit stops started. Piastri boxed on lap 20, under a Virtual Safety Car deployed a minute and a half earlier for a Lindblad-Lawson incident at Turn 5. Norris stayed out. By lap 30, worn tyres had pulled him back to fourth, and unable to wait for another VSC or SC, he boxed for MEDIUM: full pit-lane cost, no caution to hide behind, 7.8 seconds stationary, 28.3 total. He rejoined behind Bortoleto and Hadjar, managed one pass, and could never get close enough to pass Hadjar.

It was not for lack of pace. Nine of the ten fastest laps of the entire race, from any driver, are Norris’s, set chasing Hadjar over the final 12 laps of his own second stint. He closed from 10.4 seconds back on lap 33 to 1.05 seconds. He ran out of laps, not speed.

Part two

The pace was never the problem

Belgium’s fitted pace model ranks Norris third of 21 drivers this race, 0.244 seconds off the fastest fitted pace and 0.013 seconds off Leclerc in second, effectively tied for the second-quickest driver on track. Piastri, the teammate who beat him to the flag, rates seventh: 0.660 seconds off the same baseline, 0.416 seconds slower than Norris on raw pace this race. Full breakdown: Belgium race-pace page.

Norris was the faster McLaren

Close to half a second a lap faster than his own teammate this race, and he finished two places behind him. Pace is not what cost him Saturday’s third or Sunday’s charge to the lead. Something else did, twice, in the pits.

Part three

Two mistakes, and how we isolated them

The first mistake is the number everyone already quoted: 7.8 seconds sounds slow next to Piastri’s 2.8. Spa’s own pit lane backs it up: eight real stops at this track fit a positional model averaging 22.5 seconds total, and Norris ran 5.8 seconds over that, split across a slow wheel change and a lane transit that ran long on both sides of it.

The second mistake is the pit window: Norris never used the VSC window his own teammate had already used ten laps earlier. We checked how long it stayed open rather than assuming. Deployment landed a little past halfway through lap 20, and the all-clear came 14% into lap 21 for Norris, who was running the same slowed track at the same moment on his original tyres.

We isolated each mistake on its own, everyone else’s race held fixed, using our internal race simulator’s resume feature: replay Norris’s real history up to a chosen instant exactly as it happened, then Monte Carlo the rest of the race from there, 500 trials per variant.

  • Stop quality, holding the lap fixed at 30: resume right after his real stop (the real 7.8s/28.3s baked into history) against resume right before it, with the stop itself redrawn fresh from the race’s own pit-loss distribution instead of his real number. Any gap between the two isolates what the slow wheel change alone cost.
  • Stop timing, holding stop quality out of it: resume at Norris’s own start of lap 21, right after Piastri’s stop, with his real strategy (pit at 30) against a version where his stop is moved to fire immediately, lap 21. Because this resumes nine laps earlier than the first comparison, incorporating more Monte Carlo and less real history, we ran a control alongside it: same lap-21 resume point, real lap-30 strategy left untouched. If the control lands close to the lap-30-resume numbers above, the earlier resume point is not doing the work by itself, and any gap to the lap-21-stop version is really about the timing.

The control is the number that makes this comparison trustworthy. Resuming nine laps earlier than the stop-quality pair, with nothing else changed, lands at 6.65, inside a tenth of the lap-30 numbers above it. The earlier resume point is not inflating anything on its own. The gap that opens once the stop moves to lap 21, mean position falling from 6.65 to 4.80, is the timing effect on its own, not an artifact of giving the model more race to work with.

Neither effect is invented, and the smaller one should not be dismissed. The stop-quality pair really does move the mean by only 0.07 of a position across 500 trials, because 5.8 seconds is a small input against 14 more laps of ordinary racing randomness, averaged out. But 5.8 seconds of real time is not noise, and Norris finished the race 1.05 seconds behind Hadjar. An average stop instead of his real one gives back more than five times that margin. It would not have guaranteed sixth, but it puts a fight for sixth back in range, which his real stop never did. The timing mistake is still the bigger one by a wide margin: moving the same stop one lap earlier shifts the mean by 1.85 positions, and turns a driver who reaches P5 or better in about one trial out of thirty on his real strategy into one who does it in seven trials out of ten.

Bigger, not exclusive.

Part five

What cost McLaren

Both mistakes were real, and the simulator puts a number on each rather than leaving it at an impression. The slow stop cost a little. The missed VSC window cost close to two full positions, more than the entire gap between where Norris finished and where his teammate did. Nobody needed this piece to see Piastri box under the VSC while Norris stayed out; what the broadcast could not show was the size of it. Reacting to Piastri’s stop the way a one-car team reacts to its own data would have put Norris back in the group he’d spent the first twenty laps overtaking, not stuck behind Hadjar for a full second stint.

He still nearly did it the hard way. Nine of the ten fastest laps in the race, twelve laps spent closing a ten-second gap down to 1.05, a margin the slow stop alone was more than five times the size of.

The pace was never in question at Spa. The pit wall’s two calls were.

Method. Race: 2026-07-19, Belgium (Spa-Francorchamps). Pace figures in Part two come from our internal fitted pace model, isolating each driver’s pace from tyre age, fuel load, and traffic. The pit-stop ranking and the 22.5-second positional pit-loss average in Part three come from every real stop timed at Spa that day, and from a telemetry-reconstructed entry/stationary/exit model fit to eight of them. The counterfactuals in Part four resume our internal mini-sector race simulator from a real historical instant (Norris’s own lap-30 or lap-21 crossing, as stated per variant) and run 500 Monte Carlo trials of the rest of the race per variant, with every other driver’s real strategy held fixed. All four variants share the same random seed, so differences between them come from the scenario, not from which random draws happened to land where.
