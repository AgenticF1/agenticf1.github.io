Qualifying · 2026 season · Straight-line speed

Norris's straight-line deficit cost him about 0.2 seconds per lap. We measured the top four teams across the season.

McLaren says the cause can be the power unit, the chassis or the drag. We measured the result from the telemetry: in Malaysia, a gap of about 12 kW of equivalent power, worth about 0.2 seconds per lap. Then we measured the same gap for McLaren, Mercedes, Ferrari and Red Bull in every 2026 qualifying.

2026-10-04AgenticF1~20 min read

At the Bahrain Grand Prix in Malaysia, Lando Norris was slow on the straights. McLaren said that the cause is not clear yet. It can be the power unit, the chassis or the drag of the car (The Race, Motorsport.com). We wanted to know two things. How large was the straight-line gap? And how much lap time did it cost him?

We used the telemetry from every 2026 qualifying session. We compared each driver with his teammate on the same parts of the track, at full throttle and at the same speed. We did this for four teams: McLaren, Mercedes, Ferrari and Red Bull.

The answer: in Malaysia, the gap between Norris and Piastri was about 12 kW of equivalent power. That cost him about 0.2 seconds per lap on the straights. But this gap is not new. Norris was behind Piastri on the straights in 13 of 16 qualifying sessions this year. And one kW does not always cost the same time. At Spa, one kW costs almost six times more lap time than at the Hungaroring.

Part One

The question

Section 01

What McLaren said, and what we want to know

McLaren said that Norris lost speed at the end of the straights at the Bahrain Grand Prix, and before that at Baku. Team principal Andrea Stella said that the team does not blame only the power unit. The cause can also be in the chassis or in the drag of the car. Norris qualified 0.005 seconds ahead of Piastri.

Less power, more drag and a different use of battery energy all make a car slower on the straights. The telemetry shows the result, not the cause. So we measure the gap as an equivalent power: the power difference that gives the same loss of acceleration.

We ask three questions:

  1. How large was the Norris straight-line gap in Malaysia?
  2. How much lap time did it cost him?
  3. Is this gap special to this weekend? How large are the power gaps between teammates at other teams?

Section 02

Words we use

  • Full-throttle section: a part of the lap where the driver uses full throttle and no brake. Most of a section is straight, but some sections also include fast corners that the driver takes at full throttle. The track in Malaysia has six full-throttle sections.
  • Straight: a part of the track with almost no corner (radius 500 m or more).
  • Push lap: a fast qualifying lap. We do not use out-laps or in-laps.
  • Tow: the slipstream from a car ahead. A tow makes a car faster on a straight.
  • Clipping: in 2026, the battery stops giving power near the end of a long straight. The car then stops accelerating, even at full throttle.
  • Power gap: the straight-line difference between two teammates, as equivalent power in kW. It includes less power, more drag and a different use of energy. A negative value means that the first driver is slower on the straights.

The map below shows the six full-throttle sections in Malaysia. The dark parts are straight. The light parts are fast corners that the driver takes at full throttle. Section 1 is the main straight. It crosses the timing line.

Part Two

What we do

Section 03

Why lap time and top speed do not give the answer

The easy method is to compare top speeds or speed-trap values. This method does not work well, for three reasons.

First, the speed on a straight depends on the corner before it. A driver who exits a corner faster is also faster on the next straight. This is a driver effect, not a car effect.

Second, a tow adds speed. A car with a tow can be faster than a car with more power.

Third, the 2026 cars clip. The top speed often depends on the battery, not only on the engine.

The speed-trap values also change a lot. Between Norris and Piastri, the speed-trap difference changes from −5 km/h to +4 km/h across the season. One speed-trap value does not tell us much.

Step 1

We use the speed trace on every full-throttle section

The cars send their speed about four times per second, in whole km/h. The figure below shows the back straight in Malaysia. At the start of the straight, Norris is a little faster. Near the end, Norris is 3 to 5 km/h slower.

We use only push laps. We put each Norris lap next to the Piastri lap from the same run. The two cars then have the same tyres and almost the same track conditions.

We remove a section from a lap when another car is less than 2 seconds ahead on it. This removes most of the tow effect.

Step 2

We compare acceleration at the same speed

This step is the main part of the method.

A car with less power or more drag accelerates less. But a car also accelerates less when it goes faster. So we must compare the two cars at the same speed.

From the speed trace, we calculate the acceleration (see the figure below). Then we plot the acceleration against the speed. On each full-throttle section, the points make a curve. When the car clips, the curve goes to zero. We do not use the points after clipping, because the battery controls them, not the engine.

We use only the straight parts of each section. In a fast corner, the acceleration also depends on the driving line, not only on the power. A section with too little straight running is not used for the fit. In Malaysia, sections 2 and 3 are mostly fast corners, so the fit uses sections 1, 4, 5 and 6.

Each section has its own curve, because each section has a different slope, drag and gear. Both teammates share this curve. We then add one term for the first driver. This term is the power gap. The figure below shows the fit on the two longest straights. The Norris curve is a little below the Piastri curve at the same speed.

This method removes the corner exit from the comparison. A driver who exits a corner slowly only starts lower on the same curve. A car with less power or more drag is on a lower curve.

Step 3

We measure how sure we are

We do the fit again 500 times. Each time, we use a random new set of the same laps. The spread of these 500 results gives the uncertainty (see the figure below, top chart). In Malaysia, all 500 results show Norris behind Piastri.

We also do the fit on each section separately (see the figure above, bottom chart). This shows where the gap is.

Note: each session has only 3 to 6 pairs of laps. The uncertainty values are approximate.

Step 4

We change power into lap time

One kW does not give the same lap time at every track. Extra power helps only at full throttle, and only before the car clips. A track with long straights, like Spa, gives more lap time for each kW. A slow track, like Monaco, gives less.

For each track, we calculate how many milliseconds of lap time one kW gives (see the figure below). We use the same physics as in Step 2. A figure in Appendix A.5 shows where on the Malaysia lap the time comes from.

The values go from 6 ms per kW at the Hungaroring to 32 ms per kW at Spa. Malaysia is in the middle, at about 14 ms per kW.

The lap time that a power gap costs is then simple: the power gap multiplied by the value for that track.

Step 5

We check the method

We did five checks.

Check 1: a second measurement. We also measured the time on the full-throttle sections directly. For this, we removed the effect of the corner exit with a different calculation. This measurement does not use the acceleration fit. For 148 teammate sessions, we compared the two measurements (see the figure below). They agree, but with scatter. In 74% of the results, the two measurements have the same sign. The correlation is 0.54.

Check 2: the usual size of a teammate gap. We did the same analysis for every teammate pair in every 2026 qualifying session. That is 157 results. The median size of a teammate power gap is 8 kW. One result of 10 kW is therefore not special. A gap that stays on the same side for the full season is special.

Check 3: a known engine difference. In Madrid, Leclerc used the older ADUO-1 engine specification, because Ferrari wanted to check his ADUO-2 engine after his crash at Monza. Hamilton used the new ADUO-2 specification (Motorsport.com, The Race). The FIA documents do not show this change, because a return to an older engine is not a new part. Our method shows Hamilton with 19.2 kW more power in Madrid (90% range 8.5 to 30.6 kW). This is one of his two largest advantages of the season. The other is Barcelona (21.2 kW). At Monza, both Ferrari cars had the new engine. There, the method shows a gap of +1.1 kW. The method finds a known engine difference when it exists, but other weekends can show gaps of the same size.

Check 4: other model settings. We changed the acceleration window and the shape of the speed curve. The Malaysia gap changed by up to 3 kW, and the season values by up to 2 kW. No result changed sign (see Appendix A.1).

Check 5: the fast corners included. We did the fit again with all the full-throttle data, fast corners included. In Malaysia, the Norris gap is then −11.2 kW (with only the straight parts: −12.2 kW). For the season, the McLaren gap becomes smaller: −6.1 kW (90% range −9.4 to −2.7 kW). The Red Bull gap becomes −4.2 kW (90% range −8.1 to −0.4 kW). So the fast corners make the McLaren gap smaller and the Red Bull gap larger. We use only the straight parts, because there the acceleration depends on the power, not on the driving line.

Part Three

What we find

Section 09

Malaysia: a straight-line gap of about 12 kW

At the same speed, Norris accelerated less than Piastri. As equivalent power, the gap is 12.2 kW. The 90% range is 6.1 to 17.3 kW.

The gap was on almost every full-throttle section. Norris lost time on five of the six sections. His top speed was 0.4 to 2 km/h lower on five of the six sections.

At 14 ms per kW, the gap cost Norris about 0.18 seconds per lap. The direct measurement on the full-throttle sections gives 0.23 seconds per lap. The two values agree. McLaren's own estimate is 0.1 to 0.2 seconds per lap, and 1 to 2 km/h of top speed (The Race).

Norris was faster than Piastri in other parts of the lap. Over the four runs, he was on average 0.21 seconds faster in the rest of the lap. Most of this came from Q1 and from his last Q3 lap. His best lap was 0.005 seconds faster than the best lap of Piastri.

Note: on the four sections that we can fit, the gap is between 10 and 18 kW. A problem that affects the full car, like less power or more drag, gives a similar gap everywhere, and this is what we see. But each section has only a few laps, so these values have wide ranges.

Only the McLaren gap is clearly not zero. It also stays on one side more often than the gap of any other team with a full season of data. And it changes very little from weekend to weekend: the differences between sessions are about as large as the measurement noise.

Verstappen was behind his teammate in 10 of 14 sessions, but his season value is not clearly different from zero. His gaps change a lot: −31.1 kW at Silverstone and −18.0 kW at the Hungaroring, but +22.4 kW at Miami. We have no Red Bull data for Melbourne and Shanghai. A power gap can also come from a different wing level (see Part 4).

The Mercedes and Ferrari gaps are zero on average. But they change from weekend to weekend:

  • Russell had 21 to 25 kW more than Antonelli in Melbourne, Barcelona and Monza. He had 11 to 14 kW less at Monaco, Silverstone and the Hungaroring.
  • Hamilton had 6 to 9 kW less than Leclerc at Suzuka, Montreal and the Hungaroring. He had 15 to 21 kW more at Spielberg and at the two races in Spain.

Malaysia was one of the bad weekends for Norris. His gap was 4 kW larger than his season value. At this track, that is about 0.06 seconds per lap.

McLaren said that the problem started at Baku. In our data, the Norris gap was 7.5 kW at Baku and 12.2 kW in Malaysia. Over the full season, it was 8.1 kW on average. So our data shows a similar gap at most weekends, not only from Baku.

Section 11

The cost: the same gap costs different time at each track

The figure below shows the lap time that each power gap cost, session by session.

For Norris, the same gap gave very different costs:

  • At Monza, 5.7 kW cost him 0.14 seconds per lap.
  • At Monaco, 6.2 kW cost him only 0.04 seconds per lap.
  • His largest gaps were at Zandvoort (27.8 kW), Montreal (16.7 kW) and Shanghai (15.4 kW). They cost him 0.35, 0.21 and 0.22 seconds per lap.

The cost changes a lot from session to session. The gap cost Norris time in 13 of 16 sessions. The median cost is 0.12 seconds per lap. The largest cost is 0.35 seconds per lap, at Zandvoort. At Miami, Norris was faster than Piastri on the straights. There, the gap gave him 0.09 seconds per lap.

Verstappen shows the same effect. At the Hungaroring, 18.0 kW cost him only 0.11 seconds per lap. At Silverstone, 31.1 kW cost him 0.63 seconds per lap.

The figure below shows this effect without drivers. For each track, it shows the lap time that a power deficit of 5, 10 or 20 kW costs. A 10 kW deficit costs 0.06 seconds per lap at the Hungaroring. The same deficit costs 0.32 seconds per lap at Spa.

Tracks with a longer qualifying lap usually lose more time (correlation 0.65). But the share of the lap at full throttle explains the cost better (correlation 0.83). Extra power helps only at full throttle. For each 10% more of the lap at full throttle, one kW costs about 5 ms more per lap.

Part Four

Conclusion

In Malaysia, Norris had a straight-line gap to Piastri of about 12 kW of equivalent power. This cost him about 0.2 seconds per lap at full throttle. McLaren's own estimate is similar. He was faster in the corners. His best lap was 0.005 seconds faster than the best lap of Piastri.

The gap is not special to this weekend. Norris was behind Piastri on the straights for most of the season, by about 8 kW of equivalent power on average. Malaysia was a bad weekend inside this pattern. The other three teams show no clear gap over the full season, but their gaps change a lot from weekend to weekend.

The cost of a power gap depends on the track. One kW costs 6 ms per lap at the Hungaroring and 32 ms per lap at Spa. A small gap can be important at Monza and almost free at Monaco.

What the data cannot tell us

  • Power or drag. More wing also makes a car slower on the straights. The telemetry cannot show the difference between less power and more drag. McLaren also says that the cause can be the power unit, the chassis or the drag. In Malaysia, Norris was also faster in the corners. More wing can cause this.
  • Engine or battery. In 2026, the battery controls a large part of the speed on the straights. We do not use the data after the car clips, but the use of energy before that point can still be a part of the gap.
  • The kW value is an estimate. It uses a car mass of 775 kg. With a different mass, the kW value changes, but the direction and the order of the results do not change.
  • Later braking is not included. A faster car can brake a little later. Our time cost does not include this. The real cost is therefore a little higher.
  • Small data. Each session has only 3 to 6 pairs of laps. One session can give a wrong value. The season values are more reliable than the values for one session.

Appendix B

Data and method notes

  • Data: OpenF1 car_data (speed, throttle, brake, gear, about 4 samples per second) and laps for every 2026 qualifying session. The tow check uses the gap to the car ahead at about 20 points on each lap.
  • Push laps: laps within 2% of the best lap of the driver. We do not use out-laps, in-laps, or laps with a broken telemetry trace.
  • Teammates: for Red Bull, the teammate is the driver of the other car in that session (Hadjar, or Lawson at Zandvoort, Monza and Madrid).
  • Pairs of laps: we put each lap next to the lap of the teammate with the nearest start time, if the difference is less than 4 minutes.
  • Full-throttle sections: at least 75% of the laps are at full throttle with no brake, for at least 250 m. The throttle value changes between cars (99, 100 or 104 at full throttle), so we use the full-throttle level of each lap. The two parts of a section that crosses the timing line are calculated separately, because they are at the start and at the end of the lap.
  • Straight parts: track radius of 500 m or more, from the car position data of the fastest lap, smoothed over 30 m. Only these parts are used for the power fit. The lap-time calculations use the full sections, because extra power also helps in a fast corner at full throttle.
  • Tow: we do not use a section in a lap if a car is less than 2 seconds ahead on it.
