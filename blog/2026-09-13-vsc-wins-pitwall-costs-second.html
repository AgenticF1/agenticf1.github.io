Madrid · 2026 Spanish Grand Prix · Race

VSC costs Norris the win, Pitwall costs Norris the second

Lando Norris comfortably leads the race by over 6 seconds. The VSC comes out right after he has driven past the pit lane entry, and he misses the free stop under a Virtual Safety Car by a few seconds, paying close to the full price for a stop that should have been nearly free. We pulled the pace model and the race’s own spatial timing data to separate what the VSC forced on him from what his own pit crew cost him, and to check whether a cleaner stop, or simply staying out two laps longer, would have been enough to come out of the pits still ahead of Max Verstappen.

Part one

A pole, a lead, and a Virtual Safety Car

Section 01

The pole

Norris took pole the day before, and it was close: 91.824 s against Antonelli’s 91.835 s, eleven thousandths of a second, with Verstappen a tenth and a half further back in third. Leclerc qualified fifth, half a second off pole.

Pole put him at the front, and he kept it. Norris had a clean start and led into Turn 1. The position feed briefly credits Antonelli with the lead twice in the first forty seconds, but neither is a real pass: Antonelli cuts the chicane at the start, then cuts again through Turns 5 and 6, gaining track position off the racing line rather than on it. Verstappen is the one who gets past Antonelli for real, taking second off him once the field settles.

Section 02

The lead

From there the gap built the ordinary way, half a second to a second and a half a lap for most of the first stint. By lap 13, the last clean lap before anything happened, he led Leclerc by 14.0 s, Antonelli by 6.4 s and Verstappen by 12.2 s.

Pace, start to finish

Our pace-model fit puts Norris roughly 0.5–0.6 s a lap faster than all three across the race. None of the fourteen seconds needed a fight for the lead: he had it from the green light, and the gap from there is a full stint of a car that is simply quicker.

Section 03

The Virtual Safety Car

A local yellow came out first, in track sectors 22 and 23 at 13:26:41. Race control upgraded it to a Virtual Safety Car 37 seconds later, at 13:27:18. The VSC itself ran a little over two minutes before ending at 13:29:21. Leclerc stayed out through all of it. Antonelli and Verstappen both ducked into the pits while it ran.

A pit stop under a Virtual Safety Car is close to free: the whole field is capped to the same slow pace, so a car in the pits is not losing ground to cars still racing at full speed. The official gain was about 11 seconds, against the roughly 26 seconds the same stop costs under green flag. Measured against Leclerc, who took none of that gain, Antonelli and Verstappen each gave up only about 9 seconds, close to the official figure.

Norris never had the choice. By 13:27:18 he had already driven past the pit entry for that lap; the next time he reached it, at 13:29:21, the VSC had already ended. He pitted lap 15 into a green flag because the message arrived a lap too late, not because McLaren chose to leave him out.

One lap decided the discount

A lap earlier and Norris is in the same cheap window as Antonelli and Verstappen. Instead he is the only one of the three who pits into a green flag, and the timing of a VSC, not a strategy call, is why: one lap decided who got the discount and who paid full price.

Section 04

Where it stood when the VSC ended

By the moment the VSC ended, Norris was 20.0 s clear of Leclerc, about 21 s clear of Antonelli, and about 26 s clear of Verstappen, with Antonelli and Verstappen having already banked their cheap stops and Leclerc still to make his own move.

Part two

The fallout

Two things stacked against him at once. He paid the full green-flag price for his stop instead of the roughly 11-second VSC discount Antonelli and Verstappen got, and the stop itself ran slower than a normal green-flag stop on top of that. Either one alone costs track position; together they turned a 26-second lead into fifth place.

The official position feed confirms it plainly: Leclerc P1, Antonelli P2, Verstappen P3, Norris P5, the moment Norris commits to the pit lane. He comes out of the pits 6.0 s behind Verstappen and just 0.7 s behind Russell, who becomes his new neighbor on track for the laps it takes to fight back through.

A 26-second lead over Verstappen turned into fifth place inside two laps.

Part three

Would a clean stop have kept him ahead of Max?

Norris came out of the pits 6.0 seconds behind Verstappen. Take out the slow part of the stop and see where that leaves him.

A real chance, not a guaranteed pass

The two honest readings of “how slow was the stop” put a clean exit anywhere from just behind Verstappen to just ahead of him: a chance of coming out in front, not a certainty either way. More importantly, a clean stop puts Norris back into the fight laps earlier than the real one did, with way more laps of racing left to find a way past rather than a whole queue to clear from fifth. Not a clear win. A real chance at one, and more time on track to take it.

A clean stop would not have guaranteed the place back. It would have put Norris back in the fight, laps earlier, with room left to go and take it.

Part four

Can McLaren be smarter?

There was a second way to play it. Norris had been managing his tyre before the VSC, lapping at 99.2–99.4 s. It was the pace of a driver protecting a tyre with a comfortable lead. Let him push for two more laps instead of pitting on lap 15, and 99.3 s a lap is a reasonable ask. The VSC itself may even have let the tyre cool a little on the way into it.

Twenty-seven seconds is enough to cover a clean stop and still come out ahead. Piastri’s actual mid-race stop, the number McLaren already showed it can produce, cost 26.4 seconds.

Room to spare, not just a chance

Staying out two laps longer, on pure pace already on the table, projects to a bigger and cleaner margin over Verstappen than fixing the stop alone. Where scenario one gives Norris a chance at the place, this one lands with room to spare, built from real lap times the team already had in hand before the VSC was ever called.

Two laps of pace he was already capable of would have covered the pit stop with room left over.

Part five

What decided the race

The Virtual Safety Car decided this fight. That part was out of McLaren’s hands, missing the cheap window by one lap. What happened after was not.

  • A normal stop, the kind Piastri got the same afternoon, gave Norris a real chance of coming out ahead of Verstappen instead of behind him, and either way put him back into the fight laps sooner, with more laps left to attack instead of a queue to clear.
  • Two more laps at a pace already sitting in his recent lap times, no pit-stop fix required, projects to a clean margin over Verstappen with room to spare.
  • Either path also keeps him out of traffic. The stop he got put him 0.7 seconds behind Russell and starts the whole fight-back from fifth; a clean exit keeps him in the clear air he had built all race, and in position to race Antonelli directly rather than clear a lapped-looking queue first.

The slow stop is what turned a real chance to fight for the position into a clean loss of it. That part sits with the pit wall, not the Virtual Safety Car.

Part six

Could McLaren have managed Norris the way Mercedes managed Antonelli?

Antonelli said after the race that Mercedes had been managing his pace in case a Virtual Safety Car came out. His gap to Norris, however, barely moved through the lap the yellow flag fell in, 6.4 s to 6.1 s. He may have been managing the pace, just not enough to call it a real “strategic move”.

It would not have worked for Norris regardless. In the 37 seconds between the local yellow and the VSC, Norris covered only about 17% of a lap, against 34% for Antonelli and 38% for Verstappen, roughly half their pace, already. Even accounting for where he was on the track at the time, he is the one furthest around the circuit each lap by virtue of leading it, so he is the one who reaches whatever caused the yellow first, before anyone else has a reason to lift. There was no spare pace for McLaren to bank for a VSC that had not been called yet, because Norris was already paying for it by the time it was.

Method. Race: 2026-09-13, Spain (Madrid). Real gaps and lap times are measured directly from the session’s timing data: cumulative race time at each lap boundary for lap-by-lap gaps, and a telemetry-grounded spatial layer for the exact-instant gap the moment the VSC ended and the moment Norris exited the pit lane (both cross-checked against the session’s official position feed). Pit-loss figures come from the race’s own pit-stop timing data, filtered to green-flag stops only. Pace figures (the 0.5–0.6 s per lap underlying-pace edge cited in Part one) come from our internal fitted pace model, isolating each driver’s pace from tyre age, fuel load and traffic. The scenario in Part three uses two different real measurements of “how much slower the stop was” and reports both rather than picking one. The scenario in Part four uses only real, already-measured lap times and gaps, no modeled projection beyond holding a stated pace constant for two laps.
