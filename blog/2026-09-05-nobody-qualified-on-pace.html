Monza · 2026 Italian Grand Prix · Qualifying

Nobody qualified on pace

Every clean-air lap in Q3 at Monza trapped the same speed: 318 to 321 km/h, whatever the car. The 15+ km/h that spread the grid out was slipstream. So the 2026 Italian Grand Prix was not settled by who built the fastest car. It was settled by who set up the best tow. Mercedes and Alpine planned it best. Ferrari did not bother, and McLaren could not afford to. Piastri's Turn 1 lock-up is the clip everyone replayed. It is a footnote to the tow game around it. We pulled the telemetry for the whole session. Here is what it says.

Part one

The premise: this grid was a slipstream contest

Section 01

Every clean-air lap was the same speed

The whole qualifying order was a tow contest

Each bar is a driver's best main-straight trap speed in Q3. That is how fast the car was going at the speed trap before Turn 1, which at Monza is almost pure slipstream. The grey band is where every clean-air lap in Q3 landed: 318 to 321 km/h, across all four teams that ran one. In still air every car is the same speed. The tall bars are the drivers who got a tow. That, not engine, is what spread the grid out.

Bars = best Q3 trap speed (hover for the gap to the car ahead). Band = clean-air trap: Antonelli 321, Verstappen 318, Norris 318, Hamilton 320 km/h. The 15+ km/h spread on track is the tow, and it maps almost one-to-one onto grid order.

One claim carries this whole piece: at Monza a tow is worth real lap time. The test is simple. Did any driver go quicker on a clean-air run than on a towed one? In this session, none did.

Q3, every driver's two runs

Trap speed = the main-straight speed trap · “tow” vs “clean” read from trap speed and the on-track gap to the car ahead
The clean control: Verstappen

Verstappen ran two Q3 laps. On the first he sat about 2 s behind the train, trapped 326 km/h, and did 1:22.070. On the second he was alone in clean air. Trap dropped to 318 km/h and the lap fell to 1:22.381, 0.31 s slower. Almost all of the loss was in sector 1, the main-straight sector. And the slower run had ten more minutes of rubber working for it. Norris's two runs do the same thing: 323 to 318 km/h, 0.32 s slower.

What track evolution alone buys

Lindblad and Russell each trapped within 1 to 2 km/h on both runs, so their tow barely changed. Both still improved, by 0.13 s and 0.08 s, all of it in the corners. Others gained more between runs (Colapinto over half a second), so ~0.1 s is a floor on what run 1-to-2 track evolution was worth here, not a point estimate. A main-straight tow was worth 0.2 to 0.3 s: Verstappen lost 8 km/h of trap and 0.31 s; Hamilton gained 10 km/h and 0.71 s, though his first run had other problems. Track evolution does not come close to covering that.

For Piastri, that settles what a clean final run was worth. His first run (1:21.966) was towed. On the second he was the lead car, so a clean lap loses about 0.3 s of tow and claws back about 0.1 s from the track. Net roughly +0.2 s, around 1:22.15. That does not beat his own banked time, and it does not touch Gasly or Russell. His grid slot was fixed by the lap he had already done. Run 2 had no upside for him. The lock-up did not cost Piastri a lap worth having. It cost Norris the tow he was owed.

Section 2 breaks the tow down section by section. Putting an exact number on Norris's lost lap would need a full Monte-Carlo re-run of his second lap with a tow, which is out of scope here.

A tow is a trade. Less drag on the straights, less downforce in the corners. The straights: +4 to 6 km/h

On the four real straights (the run to Turn 1, the drag to Roggia, the long pull to Ascari, the back straight to Parabolica) the towed car carries 4 to 6 km/h more mean speed and 5 to 7 km/h more top speed. Both drivers, same picture. That is the low-pressure wake doing its job.

The corners: it depends how fast they are

The slow chicanes, Rettifilo and Roggia, are taken at 60 to 150 km/h on mechanical grip. There the tow is a wash, plus or minus 1 to 2 km/h of noise. The fast corners, Lesmo 2, Ascari and Parabolica, are taken at 220 to 250 km/h where aero load matters. There the towed car is 2 to 5 km/h slower. The wake has thinned the air, the front wing has less to bite on, and the driver has to ease off.

Sector 2 is the trade-off, netted out

S2 has a big straight to Ascari and also Roggia, both Lesmos and Ascari. The straight-line gain and the cornering loss come to −0.02 s and −0.00 s. They cancel. The tow's benefit lands almost entirely in S1 (about −0.20 s), which is nearly all flat-out with one slow chicane, and partly in S3 (about −0.10 s), a big straight plus one fast corner that eats some of it back.

Monza is close to the best case for a tow. The straights are more than half the lap, and only three corners are quick enough for dirty air to cost time. So the net here is a clean ~0.3 s. That is why Piastri and Norris traded runs for it, and why one broken trade is worth this much analysis.

Part two

How each team played it

Section 03

Did the front-runners have a tow, and did it help?

People say Gasly and the Ferraris did not have a tow. We can check that without a model. Look at where each car was on track and how fast it went through the speed trap. Every clean-air Q3 lap trapped 318 to 321 km/h. Anything well above that is a car in someone's wake. How many tenths the tow is worth is a harder question, because the trade between straight-line gain and cornering loss differs for a draggy McLaren and a slippery Ferrari. So we do not push the appendix's single curve per car. But whether they had a tow, and whether it was cutting their time, the trap answers cleanly.

Every Q3 flying lap: gap to the car ahead, and trap speed

Gasly: a strong tow, both runs

On the pole lap Gasly ran 2.7 s behind Hamilton and trapped 334 km/h, 15 km/h over the clean-air mark, with the gap closing through the lap. So the tow was strengthening, not fading. His first run was the same one notch weaker: 3.2 s, 331 km/h. Gasly with no tow traps around 319 like everyone else, and Sections 1 and 2 put that much main-straight speed at 0.2 to 0.3 s of lap time. The tow was cutting his time. We cannot say whether that was worth exactly one tenth or two. We can say the pole lap was a towed lap, and so was every lap near it.

Leclerc: both runs, including the second

Leclerc had a car ahead on both Q3 runs, 3.6 s then 2.9 s, and trapped 328 km/h both times, 9 km/h over baseline. The tow was there on run 2 and it was cutting his straight-line time. The lap was still 0.14 s slower than his first run, but that is the rest of the lap going backwards, 0.36 s off his own best. The slipstream worked. The driver did not put a lap together.

Hamilton: no tow run 1, a clear one run 2

Hamilton was first out on run 1 and ran alone. Trap 320 km/h, on the clean-air line, lap aborted. On run 2 he picked up a 2.5 s tow behind Leclerc. Trap jumped to 330 and the lap came down 0.7 s, to 1:22.011. His first run was scrappy, so not all of that 0.7 s is tow. But the 10 km/h of trap is. Same car, same driver, a slipstream that was not there before.

The controls: Antonelli, and Verstappen's two runs

Antonelli ran one Q3 lap, alone. Trap 321 km/h. Verstappen took a tow on run 1 (trap 326) then chose clean air on run 2. Trap fell to 318 and the lap was 0.3 s slower. Norris's abandoned-tow run 2 did the same, trap 318. Those are the reference points. A tow-free Q3 lap traps on the baseline, and it is slower.

So did Gasly, Leclerc and Hamilton have a tow on their grid-setting run?

All three, yes. In each case the trap is 9 to 15 km/h above what the same car does in clean air, so the tow was cutting their straight-line time. The idea that the front of the grid was set on pure pace does not hold up against the trap-speed column. Only Antonelli's lap was tow-free.

We cannot split each team's lap into “tow” and “car” from one session; the trade is too car-specific. What track position does say: everyone bar Antonelli had a tow on their grid-setting lap, and every one of those laps trapped well clear of the 319 km/h that clean air produces.

Trap speed = laps.st_speed, the main-straight speed trap. The clean-air band (318 to 321) is every Q3 lap with no car within about 8 s: Antonelli 321, Hamilton R1 320, Verstappen R2 318, Norris R2 318. Gap is measured at the start/finish line. Where a car towed from further up the road and then pitted (Russell, off the Antonelli lead-out) the S/F gap reads “clean” even though the trap does not.

Section 04

Where on the lap does each team spend its straight-line speed?

2026 splits the power roughly 50/50 between engine and battery. How a team harvests and redeploys that energy around the lap is one of the season's big differentiators. The deploy maps are not in public telemetry. The result is. Comparing each car's speed at the three timing traps (i1 first sector, i2 mid-lap, st the main straight) shows where its straight-line speed sits.

The main straight: where each car tops out

Speed vs % of the S/F→Turn 1 straight · Antonelli's is the only tow-free trace · the rest carry the slipstream that lifts the whole curve
The big trap-speed gaps were the tow, not grunt

Russell trapped 336 km/h and Piastri 319. Section 3 and the appendix show that spread is almost all slipstream position. In clean air, Antonelli (Mercedes), Piastri leading (McLaren) and Verstappen leading (Red Bull) all trap 318 to 322 km/h. On raw straight-line grunt the top teams are close.

The shape differs by team, and holds across Q2 and Q3

Ferrari is the most main-straight-biased. Its i2 trap sits at 96.6% of its main-straight trap, i1 at 91%. The car is slower at both mid-lap points and saves its top end for the finish-line straight, where its mean is about 327 km/h, among the highest. McLaren is the opposite, the most uniform: i2 at 99.4% of the main straight, i1 at 94%. Its speed is the same everywhere and it gets no main-straight kick. Mercedes and Red Bull sit in between, i2/st around 0.97 to 0.98.

Which is why the tow need looked different

A car with Ferrari's profile already has its top end on the main straight. Its slipstream need is mostly the run-up. A car with McLaren's flat profile has nothing extra of its own there, so it leans on the tow the whole length of the straight. That is the pattern from Sections 1 and 2, where Piastri and Norris wanted the draft all the way to the line. On the speed trace, Piastri tops out earliest of anyone, about 36% along the straight. Leclerc keeps pulling past half distance.

The top teams' straight-line speed is similar in size. They put it in different places. Ferrari front-loads the main straight. McLaren spreads it evenly and pays for that by needing a tow the length of it.

Caveats. This ratio cannot separate deployment strategy from wing level; a lower-drag rear wing gives the same signature. The intermediate traps sit after different corners and catch whatever tow the car had at that moment. And there is no ERS or battery-state channel in public data. A real read of the deploy maps needs telemetry the teams keep to themselves. What survives the caveats is the direction: Ferrari's speed is on the main straight, McLaren's is spread around the lap, and it shows up in two sessions.

Section 05

Five teams, one track, five tow tactics

Mercedes ran the most extreme tow tactic of the weekend. On both of Russell's Q3 runs a Mercedes sat 0.6 to 1.0 s directly ahead of him at the line. On the run that mattered, Antonelli drove the main straight flat out to lead Russell through, then bailed at Turn 1.

Russell's main straight: the lead-out vs an ordinary tow

Speed vs distance on the S/F→Turn 1 straight · Russell's final Q3 run (P2, Antonelli ~1 s ahead, flat out) vs his Q2 run (Antonelli 3.2 s ahead, an ordinary tow) and his first Q3 run (off Antonelli's out-lap)
It was the whole first straight, then Antonelli's lap was done

On Russell's final Q3 run, Antonelli held qualifying speed, 339 km/h at full throttle, about a second ahead for the entire S/F to Turn 1 straight. That is roughly 700 m and 8 seconds. Then he lifted and abandoned his lap into Turn 1. That is a lead-out, not a passing tow. With it, Russell peaked at 343 km/h and held that to the braking zone. On his Q2 run, with Antonelli a normal 3.2 s up the road, Russell peaked at 335 and drooped to 325 by the end. The close lead-out was worth 8 to 18 km/h. He got a similar hit on his first Q3 run, that time off Antonelli's out-lap.

Why only that straight makes sense

A sacrificial car can only tow where it is willing to drive fast. Once Antonelli bailed at Turn 1, Russell was on his own. Being towed the whole lap needs the other tactic: a team-mate running a full lap 2.5 to 3 s ahead for a moderate draft everywhere, which is what McLaren did. Mercedes instead spent one car's run on a maximal tow on the single highest-value straight, the longest flat-out stretch and the earliest, so a deficit there compounds all lap. It cost Mercedes nothing. Antonelli already had a grid penalty for changed power-unit parts and would start the race from the back whatever he qualified. Russell was fighting for the front row.

And it fits the car

Section 4 put Mercedes' straight-line profile in the middle. Not McLaren-flat, which needs a draft everywhere. Not Ferrari-front-loaded. A car like that is fine on its own for most of the lap. What it wants is a launch, and the close lead-out gives it one. It is a different answer to the same problem McLaren solved with a reciprocal tow, and at Monza the better one. Russell's 1:21.846 was P2, 0.06 s off pole.

Three teams, three tactics. McLaren traded full-lap drafts and one lap broke. Mercedes sacrificed a run for a launch and banked P2. Ferrari, whose car already has the top end, took ordinary mid-gap tows and finished about 0.2 s short on pace.

The lead-out read is firm; Antonelli's throttle and speed traces are clear. Whether Mercedes also shifted deployment toward the end of the lap, this data cannot say. The simpler reading is that the main straight is the best place to spend a sacrificial tow for any car, and Mercedes' all-round straight-line pace let them get away with needing help only there.

These tactics were not improvised. Each team's Q3 pit-exit timing, who left first, how far behind the team-mate followed, whether they paired the cars at all, is a repeatable choice, and it lines up with the car each team brought. Here is the bolder read of that.

McLaren: the reciprocal train, and they took turns paying

Run 1, Norris led Piastri out by 2.9 s. Run 2, they swapped and Piastri led Norris by 2.4 s. A full-lap mutual draft, the debt split evenly. It is the most coordinated plan on the grid and the most exposed. On run 2 the lead car locked Turn 1 and the follower lost the slipstream the instant Piastri backed out. Section 4 says why they had to. McLaren has the flattest straight-line profile of the front group: most wing, most drag, quick through the corners and slow on the straights. A car like that cannot qualify at Monza on its own, so McLaren ran the session as a team problem, two cars sharing one tow debt, rather than giving the quicker driver priority. Fair to both, and it left two or three grid slots on the table.

Mercedes: one leader, one tow machine, no reciprocity

Antonelli was released 0.6 s ahead of Russell on run 1 and 1.0 s ahead on run 2, drove the main straight flat out both times, and abandoned both laps. His one real flying lap he ran alone, P7, three tenths off, and he was starting from the back on a power-unit penalty anyway. Russell got the two highest traps of the session, 338 and 336 km/h, and qualified P2, 0.06 s off pole. This is the coldest call of the weekend. The team picked which car mattered and spent the other one's session on it. For Antonelli that session was worth almost nothing. Opposite plan to McLaren, same problem, and at this track a better result.

Alpine: the last clear slot on the most rubbered track, plus a tow

Gasly ran ahead of Colapinto every lap, 2.2 to 2.8 s, with Colapinto as the rear car. But the bigger story is where Alpine put him in the queue. On the final run Gasly was released second-to-last of all ten, 26 s after the first car and behind a five-car corridor. Hamilton sat 2.5 to 2.7 s directly ahead the whole lap, a proper tow near the optimum. Leclerc was about 5 s up, then Lindblad, Russell and Verstappen strung out to 12 s, dirty air from the whole train. Colapinto was 2.2 s behind, so Gasly was never held up and always had a target. He also ran the most rubbered-in track of the session.

Split his two Q3 laps and it shows. 1:22.001 to 1:21.786, and the 0.215 s came almost all from the cornering sectors: S2 −0.106, S3 −0.098. S1, the straight, where a tow lives, was only −0.011, with his trap up just 3 km/h. What won pole was grip and track evolution, not more slipstream. And the lap was hooked up. His S1 of 26.817 was the fastest of the final run, 0.1 s clear of Hamilton and 0.19 s clear of Russell despite Russell's much bigger lead-out tow.

So Gasly's pole was his own strong lap, plus the last clear slot on an evolving track (about 0.1 s), plus a clean Hamilton tow. Three tailwinds, and the margin to Russell was 0.06 s. Bold read: a midfield team with a shot at pole cannot out-car the front, so it played the one lever it fully controlled, run order, and sent its quick driver out last into clean air behind a ready-made tow train. High-variance play, and it landed. Calling it “a tow from Russell” is a stretch. Russell was 10.6 s up the road. Hamilton was the tow, Leclerc a marginal second.

Ferrari: the un-coordinated pair

Run 1, Leclerc slotted into the queue and Hamilton detached, running alone twenty-three seconds later. Run 2, they were released together but 2.6 s apart, a wide gap. No tow train, no sacrifice, no swap. Section 4 has Ferrari as the most main-straight-biased car in the field, so it already carries top end to the line. The tactic reads as a team that decided it had less to gain from choreography and let each driver take what the traffic gave him. Result: P4 and P5, four hundredths apart, both behind three cars that did organise a tow. The fairest approach on the grid, and probably the one that cost the most. A tow train could have put a Ferrari on the front row.

Red Bull: no partner to play with, and it did not hurt much

Verstappen took a free 1.8 s tow on run 1, then chose clean air on run 2 and lost only 0.31 s, the smallest towed-vs-clean gap of anyone. Part of that is the car; on this evidence it is the least tow-dependent at the front, good for P6 on grunt alone. But Red Bull also had no choice. Lawson was knocked out in Q2, on a final run where the FIA noted Piastri impeded him at Turn 1 (investigated post-session). So Verstappen had no team-mate in Q3 to run a train with. The Piastri lock-up was not McLaren's only cost of the day. An earlier Piastri incident is part of why Red Bull ran Q3 solo.

Same corner, same straight, five answers, and each one is the team's car talking. The draggy car builds a train and shares the cost. The mid car picks a driver and sacrifices the other. The fast-in-a-line car does not bother. The car with nothing to lose plays run order and sends its quick driver out last, into clean track behind a ready-made tow.

The trade that broke: Piastri

The brake point was not the problem

On the lock-up lap Piastri braked 168 m before the apex. That is within a couple of metres of his first run (170 m), and earlier, further from the corner, than his clean Q2 lap at 152 m. On each driver's outright fastest lap, Piastri braked about 6 m later than Norris, in the same direction and roughly the same size as other public timing data has shown. So he did not brake too late for this lap. Whether the marker itself was too aggressive is a separate question, and Section 7 shows it was 60 m later than his quickest practice reference for almost no time gain.

He arrived slower, not faster

As the lead car with no slipstream, Piastri was about 316 km/h on the approach and 301 km/h at the brake point. That is the slowest entry of any run here, about 13 km/h down on his first run. A lock-up from a slow entry at a normal brake point points to an abrupt or over-pressured application, not to carrying too much speed. Cold fronts off the out-lap are a plausible factor.

Two losses, only one of them a mistake

Against his first run, Piastri is already about 0.2 s down by the braking zone, all of it shed on the straight, running 10 km/h slower with no tow. He holds that gap level through the braking itself. Then it goes. The front locks, he releases and reapplies, apex speed collapses from about 70 to 40 km/h, and by the exit of Turn 1 he has lost 4.7 s more. Sector 1 came out at 33.16 s against his usual 27. The first loss is the price of leading the tow. The second is the error.

Section 07

Practice vs qualifying: the brake point moved 60 metres

The practice data shows a big shift. Across the weekend Piastri walked his Turn 1 brake point steadily later.

Where Piastri braked, every session

Metres before the T1 apex · longer bar = braked earlier · lock-up lap outlined · all laps Piastri except NOR Q2

His fastest FP3 lap braked 234 m before the apex. His fastest FP2 lap 196 m. By qualifying he was at about 170 m on the first run and the lock-up, and 152 m on the Q2 lap. That is a 60 to 80 m move from his quickest practice reference.

Some of that is normal. Everyone brakes later in qualifying, on low fuel and fresh softs. The point is this: the FP3 lap set nearly the same sector 1 (26.98 s against the first run's 26.93 s) while braking 60 m earlier. So the aggressive qualifying brake point was buying almost no lap time. It was spending safety margin. On the final run, with a slow entry and no tow, that margin was gone.

Caveat: the FP laps were on used tyres and likely more fuel, which lowers grip and brings the brake point forward on its own. The size of the shift is real. How much of it is Piastri getting braver and how much is the car having more grip, we cannot separate from this data.

Section 08

Skill, or luck? And should he have gone for it?

Piastri's own account, after the session:

“So the second lap, yeah, I was leading the train around… I think I was already like two tenths down into Turn 1. So I tried to compensate. Your braking point changes quite a lot with that speed difference. So I tried to probably be a bit too ambitious, locked the front and that was it.” — Oscar Piastri
Judging the quote: it holds up

Every checkable claim in it is right. He was the lead car. He was “two tenths down into Turn 1”; the Δ trace has him 0.17 to 0.20 s down on his first run by the braking zone, all of it lost on the straight with no tow. And “your braking point changes with that speed difference” is sound physics, and a sharper read than the “I braked late” line he gave first.

The one thing the data cannot confirm is the mechanism he implies, that he moved his braking point to compensate. His marker barely shifted: 168 m against 170 m before the apex, lift 176 m against 182 m. The telemetry shows a lock from a normal brake point on a slower, colder entry. That fits “too ambitious” meaning pedal pressure and commitment, which are not in the feed, rather than a later marker. Either way, his framing, down because of the tow then over-reaching to claw it back, matches the data and does not dodge the blame.

Skill or luck: this one is on the driver

The data rules out both headline explanations. He did not brake too late (Section 6). He did not carry too much speed; he was 13 km/h slower at the brake point than on his first run. His entry inputs were almost identical to that clean lap: lift 176 m against 182 m, brake 168 m against 170 m, coast 8 m against 12 m.

That is why the lock-up is hard to excuse. The one thing that changed was entry speed, 13 km/h slower with no tow. Reading brake pressure off the car underneath you is a core qualifying skill. He put a brake load calibrated for a faster, more loaded front axle onto a slower, colder one and locked it. Brake pressure and tyre temp are not in the feed, so the mechanism is inference. The gap between his inputs and the outcome is not. Nothing broke and nothing random intervened. This is a driver-adaptation error on the most important lap of the session, and a top driver is expected to make that adjustment.

There is mitigation. He was rarely in this position. His front tyres were the coldest of any of his Q3 laps. And per team radio, not this data, his practice program did not have him running as the tow-giver. That explains why it happened. It does not move it off his side of the ledger.

Was it reasonable to attack that hard? No.

Being out there leading the lap was the job. McLaren is the draggiest car at the front and its only route to the front row is the reciprocal tow train, which needs a lead car. That part is not the criticism.

The criticism is attacking Turn 1 flat out on that lap. Piastri had nothing to gain. His 1:21.966 was banked, and as the tow-less lead car his best possible run 2 was about 1:22.1. He could not improve his time or his grid slot however hard he pushed (Section 9). What he could do was bank a clean lap and hand Norris a full-lap tow, the reason he was out front. Instead he over-drove a corner that offered him nothing, locked the front, backed out, and took Norris's slipstream with him. His own quote, “a bit too ambitious”, is right, and his Q2 lap shows he knew a safer entry, 46 m of coast against 8 to 12. Pushing a lap that could not help him, in a way that wrecked his team-mate's, was a poor call as well as poor execution.

Leading was the job. Attacking Turn 1 flat out was not. Piastri's grid slot was already fixed, so the lap had no upside for him. The lock-up cost him nothing and cost Norris his only towed lap of Q3.

Section 09

What the lock-up actually cost

The two McLarens ran a reciprocal tow in Q3. Run 1, Norris crossed the line 2.9 s ahead in clean air and towed Piastri, who banked 1:21.966, provisionally P2. Norris, no tow himself, did 1:22.256. Run 2, Piastri led by 2.3 s to return the favour, locked up, and set no time. Norris, with Piastri gone by mid-lap, ran mostly clean air and did 1:22.573, 0.32 s slower than his own first run.

Cost to Piastri: essentially none

This is the part usually told wrong. He was provisionally P2 after run 1. On the final runs Gasly (1:21.786) and Russell (1:21.846) went past his banked 1:21.966. But that time stays on the board. A grid slot is a driver's best Q3 lap, and a slower run 2 cannot lose it. And run 2 was always going to be slower. As the lead McLaren he had no tow, worth about 0.3 s at Monza, so his ceiling was around 1:22.1: behind Gasly, behind Russell, and not beating his own first run. Piastri was P3 the moment Gasly and Russell went quicker, lock-up or not. The crash cost him nothing on the timesheet.

Cost to Norris: more

Norris started P9, on his run 1 time. An F1 grid is set purely on Q3 pace, so his quicker Q2 lap (1:22.067, good for about P6) never counted. Both his Q3 runs were compromised by the tow plan. On run 1 he was giving the slipstream, and his own draft off Russell drained away down the lap. Run 2, the one he was owed, went in the lock-up. His P9 is not his pace. It is the reciprocal tow failing him twice.

Appendix

A number for the tow, and how it scales

The maths

The tow as a function of the gap

A shape for the tow, and for how it scales with the number of cars ahead and how far away they are. We fit a two-term model to every competitive flying lap in the session (Q1, Q2 and Q3, within 1.5 s of the phase best), with g = seconds to the nearest car ahead at the line. It overfits one race. That is expected. The shape is what we are after.

The model

Two exponentials in the same variable, pulling against each other. The slipstream cuts drag, range τs, a few seconds. Dirty air strips downforce, range τc, shorter. We fit one from the trap-speed curve, which barely depends on lap quality, and the other from lap time, which is noisier.

How far the car ahead is

Trap speed climbs as the gap closes, from about 322 km/h in clean air to about 335 km/h right on the gearbox, e-fold range 2.6 s. That part is firm. Lap time is the shakier half. It is best around g* ≈ 3 s, net −0.14 s against clean air, and under about 1.8 s of following it turns into a net loss, where the dirty air costs more in the corners than the slipstream saves on the straights. A 2 s car and a 4 s car really are different: about 6 km/h against 3 km/h of trap, and a hair over 3 s is where the lap-time trade is best.

That −0.14 s is a conservative central estimate. Plenty of individual towed laps in the sweet spot sit near −0.4 to −0.6 s in the Δt panel. The fit is pulled back toward zero because clean-air laps scatter ±0.5 s on car quality alone, and the regression cannot tell a good tow from a good car. The same-driver controls in Sections 1 and 2 (about 0.2 to 0.3 s) are the better guide to the typical value. The best case is probably bigger than −0.14.

The gap is not constant down the lap

The model's g is the gap at the start/finish line. Everyone in the train runs a slightly different pace, so it drifts. Over these pole laps it moved by −0.5 to +0.4 s from S/F to the end of the lap. Where a car ahead was quicker, the follower's tow weakened through the lap (Norris, Colapinto, Lindblad: +0.3 to +0.4 s). Where it was slower, the follower reeled it in and the tow strengthened (Russell, Verstappen: −0.3 to −0.5 s). The per-lap average gap is a better physical measure but compresses the spread, so we keep the S/F gap in the fit and report the drift alongside each driver in Section 3.

How many cars ahead: no measurable stacking here

A pure cumulative-wake model, where every car ahead adds in full, , fits no better than nearest-only, and slightly worse. A separate second-car term () comes out at B ≈ 0, with a CI straddling zero and no R² gain. Hold the nearest gap in a 1.5 to 3.5 s band and add a second car within 5 s, and we see no extra trap speed. The lap is if anything 0.2 s slower.

A train's wakes almost certainly do add. This only says that with one session of laps, and second cars mostly 5 s or more back where any extra draft is small, the effect is below the noise floor. Take it as “the nearest gap is what matters, a distant second car adds little”, not as proof that stacking does not exist.

The trap side is firmer than the lap-time side

Trap-speed gain: A ≈ 13 km/h, 90% bootstrap CI [9, 16]. The lap-time optimum is looser. Clean-air laps scatter ±0.5 s on car quality alone, so the net benefit is −0.14 s with a CI of [−0.31, 0], and the exact shape moves with every modelling choice. The same-driver controls in Sections 1 and 2, a driver's towed run against their own clean run, are the trustworthy measure of the tow's value. This model is for its shape.

Fit it on the top 10 only, and the dirty air disappears

2026's first-year cars spread the field wide, so “within 1.5 s” still mixes a McLaren with a struggling Sauber. Refit on just the ten Q3 drivers, 54 laps. The trap curve barely moves (A ≈ 18 km/h). The lap-time model's dirty-air term collapses to zero. For the front cars the tow is close to pure gain: closest is quickest, no mid-gap optimum. Better aero headroom, better car control, or 2026 cars that shrug off turbulence when well sorted. The cornering penalty that gives the full field an optimum looks like a slower-car problem. Both curves are drawn above.

Across the 2026 qualifying calendar the per-track fits are noisy, but the pattern holds. The trap-speed gain runs from about 0 to 20 km/h, biggest at the low-downforce rounds, Monza and the Americas. The net lap-time benefit is about zero at the median track. At most circuits the slipstream and the dirty air roughly cancel. A few, Monza among them, are where the trade pays.
