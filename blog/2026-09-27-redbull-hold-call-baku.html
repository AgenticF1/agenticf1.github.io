Red Bull’s real call at Baku cost the team the position it was trying to protect

Verstappen passed Hadjar at Turn 1 on lap 7, spent the rest of the stint stuck behind Piastri, and never got through. We ran the counterfactual: what if Red Bull had held him back instead. Read the pace at face value and the answer says the team made the right call. Correct for the one thing the pace model cannot see on its own, a hold-position radio call team radio confirms really happened, and the answer flips.

2026-09-27AgenticF1

Part one

What happened

Lap 7, Turn 1. Verstappen overtakes Hadjar. A clean pass, the kind a team wants from its faster car. Then nothing. For the next twenty-one laps Verstappen sits behind Piastri’s McLaren, never inside a second at the one point on track where a run turns into an overtake, and finishes the stint exactly where he started it: stuck. Real positions at lap 28, the last clean lap before the Albon-incident Safety Car neutralizes the race: Piastri second, Verstappen third, Hadjar fourth.

Hadjar, meanwhile, kept closing. At lap 20, same corner, he comes back at Verstappen and gets to within eight thousandths of a second, close enough that we found it by accident scanning the timing data for outliers. Verstappen holds the position. But the gap that let him do it was already gone.

Part two

What the pace numbers say, taken at face value

We reran the race from that exact lap-7 instant with Hadjar’s pass never happening. Verstappen, running fresh simulations instead of replaying history, still gets past Hadjar in 38% of them. In the rest, Hadjar holds, the way he nearly did for real. We let the model run each version out to lap 28 and counted where everyone landed.

Holding Verstappen back looks like the wrong call

Across 2,000 simulated versions of the rest of the stint, the team’s chance of getting at least one car past Piastri drops from 87% (the real outcome, replayed forward) to 79% if Verstappen is held. The team’s combined finishing position gets worse, not better. Verstappen is the faster car by close to three tenths of a second a lap on the numbers Hadjar’s held SOFT-stint laps produced, and holding him back wastes more of that edge than Hadjar’s own shot at Piastri gains back.

That is the honest answer to the question as asked. It is also built entirely on one number: how fast Hadjar’s tyre really was.

Part three

The number that number gets wrong

Start with the tyre. SOFT was a significantly faster compound at Baku this weekend, worth close to three tenths of a second a lap over MEDIUM once tyre age is accounted for. That gap alone is enough to explain most of the modeled difference between the two Red Bull drivers above.

The gap may run wider still for this specific car. Verstappen raced both tyres for real at different points in the weekend. On SOFT, he ran about a quarter of a second a lap faster than his own overall race pace would predict. On MEDIUM, he ran about a tenth of a second slower. Both point the same way: this Red Bull, this weekend, seems to like the softer tyre more, and the medium one less, than the field average says it should.

Which is exactly why Hadjar’s SOFT number cannot be trusted at face value. Team radio confirms Red Bull told him to hold position for the whole of that stint, so his lap times measure the gap he was told to hold, not the pace he had. He does have one clean read on his own pace, though: a second stint, later, on MEDIUM, with nobody racing him for the place and nobody to hold. It is the only part of his race not shaped by the instruction.

Put it together and projecting his SOFT pace from that clean MEDIUM stint is a reasonable call, maybe even a cautious one. If this Red Bull really does favor SOFT the way Verstappen’s own laps say it does, Hadjar’s real gap to Verstappen on SOFT could be wider than what this projection uses, not narrower.

The projection

On his clean MEDIUM stint, Hadjar ran about 0.13 s a lap faster than expected. On the held SOFT stint, about 0.08 s a lap slower than expected. Carry that same excess pace onto his SOFT tyre instead of the number his held laps produced, and his real gap to Verstappen at the start of the stint shrinks to three hundredths of a second, close enough that the crossover point where his tyre’s shallower wear rate should put him ahead moves from lap 26 to lap 17. Nine laps earlier. Nine more laps of him being the faster car, not the one running defense.

Although Hadjar’s pace comes from nine clean laps only, the 0.13 s difference still tells a story beyond noise.

Part four

Rerunning it with his real pace

We reran the same 2,000-version counterfactual, this time giving Hadjar the pace his own unheld laps say he had. Every team number reverses.

The chance of at least one Red Bull car getting past Piastri climbs to 92%, above even the real outcome. The team’s combined finishing position improves, not worsens. Piastri, facing a driver who is now faster in fact and not just on paper, gets passed more often and more decisively: Hadjar’s own conversion rate against him climbs from 69% to 85%. Verstappen’s numbers improve too, which is the part worth sitting with. He is stuck behind his own teammate more often in this version, not less, and still comes out ahead overall, because a Hadjar who clears Piastri sooner drags the whole pair forward with him.

We did not want the answer to hinge on trusting one measured number to the millisecond, so we checked how much of that 0.13 s correction the conclusion needs to hold. The break-even point, where holding Verstappen stops being worse than what really happened, sits at about 0.014 seconds a lap, a fourteenth of what Hadjar’s own second stint measured. The reversal does not require believing the full number. It survives almost any real suppression at all.

Hadjar does not need to be anywhere near that fast for the correction to matter. We reran the hold at a few points between no correction and the full measured one. Part five

What cost Red Bull

Red Bull swapped the order seven laps into a stint that ran another twenty-one, and never revisited the call. That is not good information leading to a bad decision. The team’s own decision is the reason better information never got the chance to show up. A Verstappen who looks clearly faster because Hadjar was pulled off him after four laps of actual racing is not a measurement. It is a number the team manufactured and then treated as ground truth for the rest of the stint.

It also had a chance to notice. During the stint, the team kept checking in with Hadjar over team radio about his pace. At lap 20, the same corner the order was settled at, Hadjar closed to within eight thousandths of a second of Verstappen’s bumper. That is about as direct a signal as this sport hands a pit wall that a call made under pressure is starting to look wrong in real time. The order held anyway.

Not a bet that failed. A bet that was never placed

Corrected for what the team’s own instruction did to the numbers, holding Hadjar ahead was the decision that helps Red Bull as a team. Letting Verstappen through is the one that costs it. The team did not lose a bet it was reasonable to place. It closed off the option most likely to have paid, on the strength of four laps, then sat on that call through a lap that was all but telling it to reconsider.

There was a bigger version of this it also gave up. Picture the stint where Hadjar clears Piastri instead of the other way round, then holds him through the castle section, the tight, technical stretch of the lap where following someone costs a trailing car more time than anywhere else on the circuit. Every extra tenth Piastri spends defending there instead of extending is a tenth Verstappen gets back, in exactly the part of the lap where chasing anyone down is hardest for him. That is both Red Bull cars clearing Piastri, built by the two of them working the corner together. What happened instead was one car stuck alone behind a rival for twenty-one laps, because the team had already decided which of its drivers was allowed to be the one racing.

Method. Race: 2026-09-26, Azerbaijan (Baku). All positions and gaps for laps 7 through 28 are real, measured values from the session’s timing data and telemetry-grounded spatial layer; lap 28 is the last lap before the Albon-incident Safety Car and before any of the three drivers’ own first pit stops. The counterfactual (Parts two and four) resumes our internal mini-sector race simulator from Hadjar’s own real lap-7 crossing and runs 2,000 Monte Carlo trials of the rest of the stint per scenario; Part two lets the real Turn 1 pass stand as the baseline, Part four forces a fresh, model-drawn contest at that same crossing. Our simulator’s overtake model likely overstates Red Bull’s overtake chances slightly in this race; that overstatement is consistent across every scenario compared here, so we do not believe it changes the conclusions. Pace figures (the tyre offset in Part three, and Hadjar’s and Verstappen’s own residual pace on each compound) come from our internal fitted pace model, isolating each driver’s pace from tyre age, fuel load, and traffic; Verstappen’s and Hadjar’s residuals are each driver’s own real lap times measured against what that same model would otherwise expect of them.
